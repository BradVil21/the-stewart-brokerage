Restaurants

Health insurance for restaurants and bars

Restaurants are told no more often than any other small business we work with. Almost always the problem is how the roster was presented, not the roster itself.

Why restaurants get declined

Three things make underwriters nervous about a restaurant: turnover that runs well above other industries, a roster split between salaried management and hourly floor staff, and tipped employees whose reported income moves month to month. None of those is disqualifying. All three get handled at the plan-design stage, before a carrier ever sees the file.

The declines we get called about usually trace back to a broker who submitted the full roster as eligible employees. Every server who worked two shifts in March lands in the participation math, participation fails, and the group gets a no that it never should have received.

Defining eligibility so the plan survives

Carriers test participation against eligible employees, and you set the eligibility rules. A 30-hour weekly threshold plus a 60-day waiting period is standard, entirely compliant, and it does most of the work — it anchors the plan to your kitchen leads, managers and long-tenured servers rather than the seasonal float.

Waivers do the rest. Staff covered through a spouse, a parent up to age 26, Medicare, Medicaid or TRICARE are generally valid waivers, meaning they come out of the participation calculation entirely instead of counting as declines. In a young restaurant roster this alone routinely turns a failing number into a passing one.

For a heavily part-time operation, or one built on 1099 contractors, an ICHRA fits better than a group plan. You set a monthly allowance per person, they buy individual coverage that follows them, and there is no participation test at all.

What restaurants pay in 2026

The KFF 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey put average annual single-coverage premiums at $9,325 nationally, roughly $777 per employee per month, with small firms of 10 to 199 workers at $9,211.

Restaurant groups usually quote below that. Kitchen and floor staff skew young, use relatively little routine care, and that census shape prices well — commonly $390 to $650 per enrolled employee per month for a mid-tier plan before your contribution. Because the age curve is favourable, level-funded plans are worth quoting alongside fully insured; healthy restaurant groups frequently come in materially lower and get money back when claims run under the funded amount.

You are not required to cover dependents. Funding employee-only coverage well and leaving dependent coverage employee-paid is common, compliant, and keeps participation strong without wrecking food cost.

What coverage actually does for a restaurant

Turnover is the industry’s real expense. Recruiting, training and the productivity gap on a replacement hire adds up fast, and in most markets every restaurant within a mile is competing on wage alone.

Health coverage is harder to match and harder to walk away from. For hourly staff, an employer contribution of $300 to $450 a month toward employee-only coverage is worth more than the same money as wage, because premium contributions are not taxable income to the employee and are deductible to you. It does not eliminate turnover. It moves you out of the group people leave first.

What you need to get an accurate quote

Carriers cannot price a group from a headcount alone, and a quote built on estimates gets re-rated later, which is how employers end up with a number that moves between the proposal and the invoice. What underwriters need is short and specific.

A census: for each eligible employee, date of birth, home ZIP code, gender, and whether they will enrol as employee-only, employee plus spouse, employee plus children, or family. That is it — no names, no salaries, no medical history for a fully insured quote. For a level-funded quote, most carriers also ask each employee to complete a brief health questionnaire, which is what lets them price below the fully insured rate for a healthy team.

You will also need your legal business name and EIN, your business address, an indication of what percentage of the premium you intend to contribute, and your intended effective date. Most employers can pull all of that together in under an hour from payroll. If your payroll system exports a census file, we can usually work straight from it.

The annual window that waives participation rules

If a carrier has told you no because too few employees would enrol, or because you cannot meet the contribution minimum they demand, there is one route back in and it runs on a fixed calendar.

Every year between 15 November and 15 December, fully insured small-group carriers must accept eligible small employers applying for a 1 January effective date, regardless of participation or contribution levels. The two rules that block most small groups simply do not apply during that month.

Everything else still holds — you must be a legitimate small employer, documentation must be complete, and carrier deadlines inside the window are strict and not negotiable. If you were declined earlier in the year, that is when to come back, and October is when to start assembling the census rather than the first week of December.

How long the whole process takes

From first conversation to active coverage is usually two to four weeks, and most of that is waiting on people rather than on carriers. The shape of it is consistent: a short call to confirm eligibility and what you want to spend, one to three business days to assemble quotes across the panel, a review conversation, then enrolment.

Enrolment itself is the step that slips. Every eligible employee must either enrol or sign a waiver, and chasing the last two signatures reliably takes longer than everything before it combined. Carriers set hard cut-off dates ahead of each effective date — commonly around the 15th to the 20th of the month before — and missing one pushes you a full month.

The practical implication: if you want coverage effective on the first of next month, start now rather than in three weeks. If you are aiming at a 1 January effective date through the annual special enrolment window, start in October.
