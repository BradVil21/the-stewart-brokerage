Health insurance for dental and medical practices

Practices are among the easiest small groups to place and among the most often overpaying. Small, stable, well-compensated teams are exactly what carriers want — which is leverage most practice owners never use.

Why practices quote well

A dental or medical practice usually presents underwriting with the ideal small group: six to twenty-five employees, very low turnover, professional staff, and near-universal participation because the team understands the value of coverage and actually wants it.

That profile earns competitive pricing. It also means practice owners are frequently the ones who never shop — the plan renews on autopilot for years, the increases compound, and by year five they are paying substantially above market for a group that should be getting the best rates available.

Owner and partner eligibility

How the practice is structured decides whether the owner can be on the group plan. W-2 owners of a professional corporation are generally eligible employees and enrol normally. Partners in a partnership and members of an LLC taxed as a partnership are usually not W-2 employees, which changes the analysis — and changes the tax treatment of the premium.

Sole practitioners with at least one W-2 employee besides themselves generally qualify for a small group plan in Florida, Texas and Nevada. An owner-only practice with no employees usually does not, though there are exceptions worth checking.

This is a question for your CPA alongside your broker, because the answer affects both eligibility and deductibility. Get it settled before the census goes out.

What practices pay in 2026

The KFF 2025 survey put average single coverage at $9,325 per year, roughly $777 per employee per month, with small firms at $9,211.

Practices typically sit at or slightly above the middle for 2026 — commonly $460 to $780 per enrolled employee per month before employer contribution. The driver is age: a practice with two partners in their fifties and a hygienist in her forties has a very different age curve than a restaurant, and small-group rates are built on age.

Where practices tend to win is plan tier and network. Staff who work in healthcare have strong opinions about provider access and will use the plan properly, which makes a broad-network fully insured plan a defensible spend. Level-funded is still worth quoting, but an older census is exactly where fully insured often holds up better.

Shopping a renewal you have never shopped

If your plan has renewed without being marketed for three or more years, the single highest-value hour you will spend this year is putting your census in front of the full panel. Carriers do not price loyalty, and a group with your profile is one others actively want.

Ask for renewal figures 90 days out rather than 30. With three months you can market the group, run alternate plan designs, and negotiate. With three weeks you can only accept or decline.

What you need to get an accurate quote

Carriers cannot price a group from a headcount alone, and a quote built on estimates gets re-rated later, which is how employers end up with a number that moves between the proposal and the invoice. What underwriters need is short and specific.

A census: for each eligible employee, date of birth, home ZIP code, gender, and whether they will enrol as employee-only, employee plus spouse, employee plus children, or family. That is it — no names, no salaries, no medical history for a fully insured quote. For a level-funded quote, most carriers also ask each employee to complete a brief health questionnaire, which is what lets them price below the fully insured rate for a healthy team.

You will also need your legal business name and EIN, your business address, an indication of what percentage of the premium you intend to contribute, and your intended effective date. Most employers can pull all of that together in under an hour from payroll. If your payroll system exports a census file, we can usually work straight from it.

The annual window that waives participation rules

If a carrier has told you no because too few employees would enrol, or because you cannot meet the contribution minimum they demand, there is one route back in and it runs on a fixed calendar.

Every year between 15 November and 15 December, fully insured small-group carriers must accept eligible small employers applying for a 1 January effective date, regardless of participation or contribution levels. The two rules that block most small groups simply do not apply during that month.

Everything else still holds — you must be a legitimate small employer, documentation must be complete, and carrier deadlines inside the window are strict and not negotiable. If you were declined earlier in the year, that is when to come back, and October is when to start assembling the census rather than the first week of December.

How long the whole process takes

From first conversation to active coverage is usually two to four weeks, and most of that is waiting on people rather than on carriers. The shape of it is consistent: a short call to confirm eligibility and what you want to spend, one to three business days to assemble quotes across the panel, a review conversation, then enrolment.

Enrolment itself is the step that slips. Every eligible employee must either enrol or sign a waiver, and chasing the last two signatures reliably takes longer than everything before it combined. Carriers set hard cut-off dates ahead of each effective date — commonly around the 15th to the 20th of the month before — and missing one pushes you a full month.

The practical implication: if you want coverage effective on the first of next month, start now rather than in three weeks. If you are aiming at a 1 January effective date through the annual special enrolment window, start in October.
