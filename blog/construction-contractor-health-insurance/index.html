Construction

Health insurance for construction companies

Contractors carry a stable office core and a field roster that breathes with the backlog. That shape is entirely quotable — once eligibility is drawn around the people who stay.

The two-roster problem

Almost every contractor we quote has the same structure: a permanent group — owners, PMs, estimators, superintendents, office staff, senior trades — and a variable field group that expands and contracts with work won. Underwriters looking at the combined headcount see instability and price for it, or decline.

The fix is not complicated. Eligibility gets set at 30 hours per week with a waiting period, so the plan anchors to the permanent group. Field labour that turns over between jobs never enters the participation calculation. Your core team gets real coverage and the underwriting file looks like what it actually is: a stable group.

W-2 versus 1099, and why it matters here

Construction runs on subcontractors more than most industries, and genuine 1099 subs are not your eligible employees. They cannot go on your group plan, and they do not count in your participation math — which usually helps rather than hurts.

Where it gets serious is misclassification. If workers are treated as 1099 but function as employees, that is a wage-and-hour and tax exposure well beyond benefits, and it is an area where construction draws attention from regulators. We are not the people to resolve that — your CPA and employment counsel are — but it needs to be settled before a census goes to underwriting, because the census is a representation.

For firms that genuinely run on subs, an ICHRA is often the better structure: a fixed monthly allowance per person, individual coverage that travels between jobs, and no participation test.

What contractors pay in 2026

Against the KFF 2025 national benchmark of $9,325 per year for single coverage — about $777 per employee per month — contractor groups usually land at or below the middle.

For 2026 planning, expect roughly $400 to $680 per enrolled employee per month for a mid-tier plan before employer contribution. Field crews tend to be younger than office-heavy businesses, which prices well, and that same profile makes level-funded plans worth serious consideration — unused claims funding comes back at year end.

Occupation classification does not work the way people expect in health insurance. Unlike workers’ comp, small-group medical is community rated by age and geography in the ACA market, not by trade risk. A roofing company and an accounting firm with identical censuses get very similar medical quotes.

Crews working across state lines

Contractors chasing work across a metro or a state line hit network problems fast. A plan built around one county can leave a crew working two hours away effectively uncovered for anything but emergencies.

Two clean answers: a carrier with a genuine nationwide PPO tier, which costs more but travels; or an ICHRA, where each employee buys coverage in the market where they actually live. Which one fits depends on whether your people relocate with the work or commute to it.

What you need to get an accurate quote

Carriers cannot price a group from a headcount alone, and a quote built on estimates gets re-rated later, which is how employers end up with a number that moves between the proposal and the invoice. What underwriters need is short and specific.

A census: for each eligible employee, date of birth, home ZIP code, gender, and whether they will enrol as employee-only, employee plus spouse, employee plus children, or family. That is it — no names, no salaries, no medical history for a fully insured quote. For a level-funded quote, most carriers also ask each employee to complete a brief health questionnaire, which is what lets them price below the fully insured rate for a healthy team.

You will also need your legal business name and EIN, your business address, an indication of what percentage of the premium you intend to contribute, and your intended effective date. Most employers can pull all of that together in under an hour from payroll. If your payroll system exports a census file, we can usually work straight from it.

The annual window that waives participation rules

If a carrier has told you no because too few employees would enrol, or because you cannot meet the contribution minimum they demand, there is one route back in and it runs on a fixed calendar.

Every year between 15 November and 15 December, fully insured small-group carriers must accept eligible small employers applying for a 1 January effective date, regardless of participation or contribution levels. The two rules that block most small groups simply do not apply during that month.

Everything else still holds — you must be a legitimate small employer, documentation must be complete, and carrier deadlines inside the window are strict and not negotiable. If you were declined earlier in the year, that is when to come back, and October is when to start assembling the census rather than the first week of December.

How long the whole process takes

From first conversation to active coverage is usually two to four weeks, and most of that is waiting on people rather than on carriers. The shape of it is consistent: a short call to confirm eligibility and what you want to spend, one to three business days to assemble quotes across the panel, a review conversation, then enrolment.

Enrolment itself is the step that slips. Every eligible employee must either enrol or sign a waiver, and chasing the last two signatures reliably takes longer than everything before it combined. Carriers set hard cut-off dates ahead of each effective date — commonly around the 15th to the 20th of the month before — and missing one pushes you a full month.

The practical implication: if you want coverage effective on the first of next month, start now rather than in three weeks. If you are aiming at a 1 January effective date through the annual special enrolment window, start in October.
